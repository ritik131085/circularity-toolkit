About the Toolkit

One toolkit for the full material recovery system

Circularity initiatives often underperform because one part of the system is designed in isolation — a facility without reliable feedstock, collection without quality control, recovery without markets, or scale without viable economics.

Circularity Toolkit brings the critical decisions into one place. It combines practitioner guidance, diagnostics and practical tools to help teams build new systems, improve operating facilities and scale models that work.

What makes a circular system work

Six connected layers determine system performance

Six interdependent layers determine whether material is actually recovered, valued and kept in circulation. A weakness in one can limit the performance of all the others.

01

Participation

Households, institutions and businesses separate material consistently enough to protect downstream quality.

02

Collection

Material reaches the recovery system reliably, at sufficient volume and with manageable contamination.

03

People

Workers have safe conditions, clear roles, useful skills and a meaningful place in the formal operating system.

04

Infrastructure

Facilities, equipment and logistics are sized around real feedstock, throughput and operating conditions.

05

Markets

Recovered materials meet buyer specifications and move quickly into stable downstream channels.

06

Economics & governance

Roles, incentives, contracts, revenue streams and accountability align around long-term performance.

Core principle

Do not start with the facility

Start with material flows, collection behaviour, workforce realities, downstream demand and institutional ownership. Then design infrastructure around the system you can actually operate.

What an MRF operator should know now

The Solid Waste Management Rules, 2026 superseded the 2016 rules and took effect on 1 April 2026.

Four-stream segregationWet, dry, sanitary and special-care waste are separated at source.
MRF registrationEntities sorting recyclable/non-recyclable waste at MRFs must register with the local body.
Trained personnelMRFs are required to engage trained personnel for operations.
Registered channelsSorted outputs are to move to registered processors/recyclers; required registrations matter.
Quarterly reportingReceived, sorted and channelised quantities must be reported through the online portal.
SWM Rules 2026 · MoEFCC CPCB SWM Portal MoHUA MSWM Manual · Part II MoHUA Circular Economy in Waste Management
01Context & governanceOwnership · registration · roles · incentives+

Choose the operating model only after clarifying who controls assets, feedstock, service obligations, material value and performance accountability.

Decision guideWhat to decide
  • Who owns the site, building and equipment?
  • Who is responsible for collection, sorting, residue disposal and downstream sales?
  • Which revenue streams belong to the operator, local body or partner?
  • What happens when tonnage, quality or payments fall below plan?
Operating practiceWhat good looks like
  • Use a single accountability map for land, feedstock, operations, sales and reporting
  • Tie operator incentives to controllable KPIs rather than only tonnes handled
  • Make residue-disposal responsibility and cost explicit
  • Avoid models where an operator carries market risk without control over feedstock quality
Regulatory / evidenceWhat is grounded
  • Under SWM Rules 2026, an entity sorting recyclable/non-recyclable waste at an MRF must be registered with the local body
  • MRFs must operate in line with CPCB/MoHUA guidance and deal only with entities whose registration is mandated under the rules
  • Local bodies may determine the cost of MRF services through due procedure
Evidence base: SWM Rules 2026 · MoEFCC · CPCB SWM Portal
02Feedstock & collectionFour-stream segregation · source quality · routing · intake+

Feedstock is the first operating variable. Design around what reliably reaches the system — not only what the catchment theoretically generates.

Decision guideWhat to decide
  • Which sources contribute dry waste: households, bulk generators, commercial establishments or feeder centres?
  • What share arrives correctly segregated, wet-contaminated or mixed with special-care waste?
  • How variable are volumes by weekday, season and event?
  • What load-grading, rejection and feedback rules should apply at intake?
Operating practiceWhat good looks like
  • Maintain source-wise tonnage and quality data
  • Use simple A/B/C intake grades linked to operating action
  • Separate upstream behaviour problems from collection-service problems
  • Protect dry-waste quality during transport; do not mix streams after segregation
Regulatory / evidenceWhat is grounded
  • SWM Rules 2026 mandate four-stream source segregation: wet, dry, sanitary and special-care waste
  • Dry waste is to be routed to registered MRFs or processing facilities
  • Collection and transport systems must prevent intermixing; workers involved in collection/transport are to receive appropriate PPE and training
Evidence base: SWM Rules 2026 · MoEFCC · CPCB SWM Portal
03Facility & infrastructureCapacity · layout · circulation · storage · weather resilience+

Facility design should protect linear material flow, safe movement, storage discipline and dispatch — while leaving room for realistic peak loads and maintenance.

Decision guideWhat to decide
  • What is the secured inbound TPD, not just installed capacity?
  • How much space is needed for intake, sorting, baling, finished-goods storage, rejects, utilities and circulation?
  • Can collection and dispatch vehicles enter, turn and queue without blocking processing?
  • How will drainage, rain ingress, ventilation, fire safety and emergency access be handled?
Operating practiceWhat good looks like
  • Design one-way flow from intake to sorted storage to dispatch
  • Keep finished-goods storage out of sorting and vehicle circulation zones
  • Build enough covered storage to prevent quality loss during delayed dispatch
  • Use mechanisation only where feedstock, maintenance capability and throughput justify it
MoHUA planning bandIndicative mode
5–10 TPDManual
10–100 TPDSemi-automatic
>100 TPDAutomatic
Regulatory / evidenceWhat is grounded
  • MoHUA guidance classifies MRFs broadly as manual for about 5–10 TPD, semi-automatic for about 10–100 TPD and automatic above 100 TPD
  • These bands are planning guidance, not engineering design standards
  • SWM Rules 2026 require local bodies to provide MRF/secondary-storage capacity with sufficient space for recyclables
Evidence base: MoHUA MSWM Manual · Part II · MoHUA Circular Economy in Waste Management · SWM Rules 2026 · MoEFCC
04Workforce & inclusionRoles · training · safety · retention · formal participation+

MRF performance depends on a stable, skilled and safe workforce. Staffing design must cover both productivity and worker protection.

Decision guideWhat to decide
  • What roles are required by shift: intake, sorters, baler, loader, supervisor, records and maintenance?
  • Which critical tasks currently depend on one trained person?
  • What onboarding, refresher and cross-training is required?
  • How are worker safety, grievance resolution, welfare and progression handled?
Operating practiceWhat good looks like
  • Cross-train at least one backup for critical equipment and supervisory roles
  • Use visual work instructions and daily start-of-shift checks
  • Track absenteeism, turnover, near misses and training completion alongside productivity
  • Design inclusion of waste pickers as part of the operating model, not as a separate CSR activity
Regulatory / evidenceWhat is grounded
  • SWM Rules 2026 require trained personnel for operation of MRFs
  • The Rules also assign skill-development responsibilities for waste-management manpower
  • ILO guidance supports practical action checklists covering storage/handling, workstations, machinery/electricity, physical environment, welfare and work organisation
Evidence base: SWM Rules 2026 · MoEFCC · ILO worker safety action manual
05Processing & technologyPre-sort · separation · baling · uptime · maintenance+

Technology should solve a defined material-flow problem. The processing line must be understandable, maintainable and balanced at each stage.

Decision guideWhat to decide
  • Where does the current line choke: intake, pre-sort, conveyor, category sorting, baling or dispatch?
  • Which separation steps require manual judgement and which can be mechanised?
  • What machine uptime and preventive-maintenance routines are needed?
  • What spares or service dependencies could stop the line for days?
Operating practiceWhat good looks like
  • Pre-sort bulky and contaminated material before finer separation
  • Balance each sorting station to avoid bottlenecks moving downstream
  • Maintain an asset register, preventive-maintenance calendar and critical-spares list
  • Track downtime by cause, not only total uptime
Regulatory / evidenceWhat is grounded
  • MoHUA’s municipal solid-waste manual describes MRF unit processes including pre-sorting, screening, ferrous separation, air classification, non-ferrous separation and detect-and-route systems
  • The appropriate combination depends on facility complexity, labour, equipment and cost
  • Mechanisation is therefore a design choice, not an automatic sign of better performance
Evidence base: MoHUA MSWM Manual · Part II
06Quality & traceabilitySorting standard · storage · bale tagging · special-care waste+

Quality is created through repeated checkpoints — at intake, sorting, storage, baling and dispatch — not by inspecting finished material once.

Decision guideWhat to decide
  • What does acceptable contamination look like for each priority material?
  • How should grades be labelled and stored so material cannot be mixed later?
  • What information must follow a bale or dispatch batch?
  • What happens when sanitary, special-care, e-waste or hazardous items appear in the dry stream?
Operating practiceWhat good looks like
  • Use visual category standards at sorting points
  • Use marked storage bays, FIFO and batch/bale identifiers
  • Quarantine non-conforming or special-care material immediately
  • Close the loop from buyer complaints back to sorter training and source feedback
Regulatory / evidenceWhat is grounded
  • SWM Rules 2026 create separate sanitary and special-care waste streams, reducing the case for treating these as ordinary dry waste
  • MRFs are required to manage hazardous waste from their activities under applicable hazardous-waste rules
  • MRFs may act as deposition centres for certain separately regulated waste streams where permitted
Evidence base: SWM Rules 2026 · MoEFCC
07Markets & offtakeBuyer requirements · inventory · dispatch · price discovery+

Material recovery is incomplete until the output can move into a reliable downstream channel at the required specification and payment terms.

Decision guideWhat to decide
  • Which materials drive most of the value and which create most of the inventory risk?
  • How many active buyers exist for each priority stream?
  • Who bears freight, quality rejection and payment-delay risk?
  • What dispatch frequency is needed to avoid storage congestion and working-capital lock-up?
Operating practiceWhat good looks like
  • Maintain at least an active primary and backup route for material streams where feasible
  • Track inventory age, realised price, rejection and payment days by buyer
  • Sell to specification rather than only to the highest quoted spot price
  • Use buyer feedback as an operating-quality input
Regulatory / evidenceWhat is grounded
  • SWM Rules 2026 require sorted recyclable/non-recyclable waste to be channelised to registered processing or recycling entities
  • MRFs must not deal with entities whose required registration is absent
  • Registered MRFs may serve as uptake or deposition points for EPR-linked streams where allowed
Evidence base: SWM Rules 2026 · MoEFCC · CPCB SWM Portal
08Economics & viabilityRevenue stack · unit cost · working capital · service value+

A viable MRF is usually a service-and-material business, not simply a scrap-trading business. Make every subsidy, service payment and cross-subsidy visible.

Decision guideWhat to decide
  • What is earned per inbound tonne from service/gate fees?
  • What is earned per recovered tonne from material sales?
  • What does it cost to collect, sort, bale, store, transport and dispose of rejects?
  • How much working capital is locked in inventory and receivables?
Operating practiceWhat good looks like
  • Compare economics on both inbound tonnes and recovered tonnes
  • Separate controllable operating cost from externally imposed logistics or disposal cost
  • Stress-test recovery rate, utilisation, price and service-fee assumptions
  • Treat grants or catalytic support as buying a defined capability or transition, not hiding a permanent operating gap
Regulatory / evidenceWhat is grounded
  • SWM Rules 2026 explicitly allow local bodies to determine the cost of MRF services and collected waste through due procedure
  • This reinforces a service-value model rather than relying only on material sales
  • The toolkit’s break-even and margin outputs remain planning heuristics, not statutory benchmarks
Evidence base: SWM Rules 2026 · MoEFCC
09Health, risk & complianceKPI discipline · portal reporting · registered channels · early warning+

A facility can process tonnes and still be unhealthy. Performance management should reveal whether the real constraint is feedstock, recovery, people, markets, cash flow or compliance.

Decision guideWhat to decide
  • Which 8–12 KPIs are reviewed every month and who owns action against each?
  • Which indicator should trigger immediate escalation?
  • Can intake, sorted output, rejects and dispatch be reconciled?
  • Are permits, registrations, buyer status and required reports current?
Operating practiceWhat good looks like
  • Review utilisation, recovery, inventory age, buyer diversity, downtime, productivity, safety and unit economics together
  • Use red/amber/green thresholds as screening tools, then investigate root cause
  • Maintain one compliance calendar and one evidence repository
  • Record near misses and data anomalies before they become incidents or audit findings
Regulatory / evidenceWhat is grounded
  • MRFs must report quantities received, sorted and channelised through the online portal on a quarterly basis
  • Reporting is due by the 15th of the first month of the next quarter under SWM Rules 2026
  • The health-check thresholds used on this website are working screening thresholds, not CPCB statutory performance standards
Evidence base: SWM Rules 2026 · MoEFCC · CPCB SWM Portal
10Scale & replicationReadiness · feedstock · value addition · geography · financing+

Scale a proven capability, not a weak operating system. Growth should strengthen the model’s economics, market access or service reach.

Decision guideWhat to decide
  • Is the current operation stable enough to absorb additional complexity?
  • Should growth come from more feedstock, better recovery, higher-value processing, new services or new geography?
  • Would hub-and-spoke aggregation improve logistics and utilisation?
  • What contracts, data and financing evidence will partners need before committing?
Operating practiceWhat good looks like
  • Stabilise baseline KPIs before adding capacity
  • Scale one constraint at a time and track whether system economics improve
  • Standardise SOPs, data definitions and commercial templates before replication
  • Use cluster or hub-and-spoke models only when logistics and feedstock density support them
Regulatory / evidenceWhat is grounded
  • SWM Rules 2026 allow registered MRFs to provide uptake/deposition/drop-off services for specified waste streams and EPR-linked pathways where possible
  • Urban and rural local bodies are required to establish MRF capacity based on waste generation and may use third parties/PPP routes
  • This creates a stronger regulatory basis for interoperable recovery networks, subject to local approvals and applicable waste-stream rules
Evidence base: SWM Rules 2026 · MoEFCC · CPCB SWM Portal

Risk lens

Manage risk before it disrupts performance

Use these six risk categories alongside the operating KPIs to spot issues before they disrupt throughput, safety, cash flow or trust.

OP

Operational risk

Workflow congestion, downtime and intake-output imbalance.

Watch forBacklogs, repeated bottlenecks and one-machine dependency.Respond withFlow review, maintenance discipline and labour rebalancing.
INT

Integrity & data risk

Weak records, unverifiable flows and reconciliation gaps.

Watch forMismatch between weighment, storage and dispatch records.Respond withDaily reconciliation and simple batch traceability.
SAF

Safety risk

Fire, injury, unsafe handling and poor housekeeping.

Watch forBlocked exits, hazardous items and unlogged near misses.Respond withDaily checks, isolation protocols and corrective-action closure.
FIN

Financial risk

Price volatility, cash-flow pressure and buyer concentration.

Watch forSlow receivables, old inventory and single-buyer dependence.Respond withBuyer diversification and tighter cash-cycle tracking.
COMP

Compliance risk

Licensing, labour, documentation and reporting gaps.

Watch forExpired permits or records that cannot be produced quickly.Respond withA compliance calendar and standard document repository.
SOC

Social & labour risk

Turnover, exclusion, grievances and workforce instability.

Watch forAbsenteeism, wage disputes and parallel informal practices.Respond withRole clarity, grievance channels, welfare and worker inclusion.
Evidence base

Built on current rules and practitioner guidance

How to read the toolkit

Regulatory requirements are drawn from current government rules or official guidance. Evidence-informed practices translate official manuals and worker-safety guidance into operating routines. Working thresholds are diagnostic or planning aids and must be calibrated before investment or compliance decisions.
