About this toolkit

  • Corporate sustainability, EPR and CSR teams

Core principle

Do not start with the facility.

Start with material flows, source behaviour, collection reliability, workforce realities, downstream demand and institutional ownership. Then design infrastructure around the system you can actually operate.

1
Start with the system

Diagnose the whole value chain before selecting infrastructure.

2
Find the binding constraint

Volume, contamination, people, machinery, buyers or cash flow may be the real issue.

3
Scale only what works

Replication should strengthen operating discipline rather than amplify weak foundations.

What makes a circular system work

A circular system is more than recovery infrastructure

High-performing models connect participation at source, inclusive livelihoods and integrated collection–processing–market systems.

Participation & behaviour

Build consistent segregation habits, feedback loops and service reliability so cleaner material reaches the recovery system.

People & inclusion

Strengthen safety, skills, worker dignity, formal participation and career pathways so the system is resilient and inclusive.

Infrastructure & markets

Connect collection, processing, quality, logistics and downstream markets so recovered material stays in productive use.

Practitioner modules

Ten modules cover the full circularity system

Open the area relevant to your decision. Each module translates the topic into practical questions, operating choices and signs of a healthy system.

01Context & governanceOwnership · collection responsibility · incentives+

Choose an operating model that matches institutional capacity, feedstock control and revenue logic.

Key decisions
  • Who owns assets and material value?
  • Who controls collection and feedstock?
  • Public, private, PPP O&M, concession, hybrid or cooperative?
What good looks like
  • Responsibilities are explicit
  • Feedstock commitments are clear
  • Revenue and residue risks have owners
02Feedstock & collectionSources · segregation · seasonality · intake+

Understand what material arrives, in what condition and why—before sizing a recovery facility.

Key decisions
  • Households, BWGs, commercial and feeder routes
  • Segregation and community engagement
  • Seasonal peaks and variability
  • Gate grading, intake records and rejection protocols
What good looks like
  • Source categories are mapped
  • Feedstock calendar is maintained
  • Loads are graded and routed at intake
03Facility & infrastructureSite · layout · storage · drainage · utilities+

Design the facility around linear movement, storage discipline, vehicle access and real operating conditions.

Key decisions
  • Covered footprint and storage capacity
  • Brownfield versus greenfield constraints
  • Vehicle circulation and dispatch access
  • Drainage, ventilation and monsoon resilience
What good looks like
  • No backtracking material flow
  • Storage stays out of circulation paths
  • Site works during peak weather
04Workforce & inclusionRoles · staffing · training · payment · dignity+

Build workforce resilience through role clarity, cross-training, safe work and meaningful inclusion.

Key decisions
  • Headcount by role and throughput
  • Collector, sorter and operator training
  • Backup skills for critical roles
  • Payment, formalisation and recognition models
What good looks like
  • No single trained-worker dependency
  • Safety and welfare basics are visible
  • Retention is actively managed
05Processing & technologyWorkflow · mechanisation · maintenance · uptime+

Choose the simplest processing system that can reliably deliver the required throughput and quality.

Key decisions
  • Manual, conveyor or advanced sorting
  • Wet contamination and bottleneck management
  • Preventive maintenance and spare parts
  • Machine ownership and uptime tracking
What good looks like
  • Sequential workflow
  • Low unplanned downtime
  • Technology matches feedstock and skills
06Quality & traceabilitySorting standards · storage · bale tags · special materials+

Build quality control into each stage rather than relying on one final inspection.

Key decisions
  • Material reference standards
  • Storage zones and FIFO logic
  • Bale and batch traceability
  • Sharps, e-waste and hazardous handling
What good looks like
  • Clear accepted/rejected standards
  • Traceable dispatch batches
  • Buyer complaints trigger retraining
07Markets & offtakeBuyers · dispatch · inventory · price discovery+

Treat downstream market management as an operating function, not an occasional sales activity.

Key decisions
  • Buyer diversity by material
  • Full-load and logistics economics
  • Payment terms and working capital
  • Inventory turnover and price discovery
What good looks like
  • Material does not sit for long
  • Priority streams have buyer options
  • Price realization is reviewed routinely
08Economics & viabilityCAPEX · OPEX · material value · service fees+

Make the revenue stack, cost structure and viability gap explicit before scaling.

Key decisions
  • Material revenue versus service fees
  • Cost per inbound and recovered tonne
  • Utilisation and recovery sensitivities
  • Where catalytic support is justified
What good looks like
  • Hidden subsidies are visible
  • Breakeven levers are understood
  • Growth is grounded in unit economics
09Health, risk & complianceKPIs · documentation · safety · early warnings+

Track whether the whole system is healthy—not only whether tonnes are being processed.

Key decisions
  • Operational and recovery indicators
  • Workforce and financial health
  • Compliance and traceability records
  • Risk signals and escalation thresholds
What good looks like
  • KPIs drive action
  • Near-misses are recorded
  • Documentation is current and retrievable
10Scale & replicationFeedstock · materials · services · clusters · financing+

Scale an existing capability rather than scaling ambition alone.

Key decisions
  • More feedstock or higher material value?
  • New materials, services or geographies?
  • Hub-and-spoke or cluster expansion?
  • What contracts and financing enable replication?
What good looks like
  • Current operation is demonstrably healthy
  • Scale pathway matches core capability
  • Replication simplifies rather than complicates

Risk lens

Do not wait for visible failure

Use these six categories as an early-warning checklist alongside the health indicators.

OP

Operational risk

Workflow congestion, downtime and intake-output imbalance.

Watch forBacklogs, repeated bottlenecks and one-machine dependency.Respond withFlow review, maintenance discipline and labour rebalancing.
INT

Integrity & data risk

Weak records, unverifiable flows and reconciliation gaps.

Watch forMismatch between weighment, storage and dispatch records.Respond withDaily reconciliation and simple batch traceability.
SAF

Safety risk

Fire, injury, unsafe handling and poor housekeeping.

Watch forBlocked exits, hazardous items and unlogged near misses.Respond withDaily checks, isolation protocols and corrective-action closure.
FIN

Financial risk

Price volatility, cash-flow pressure and buyer concentration.

Watch forSlow receivables, old inventory and single-buyer dependence.Respond withBuyer diversification and tighter cash-cycle tracking.
COMP

Compliance risk

Licensing, labour, documentation and reporting gaps.

Watch forExpired permits or records that cannot be produced quickly.Respond withA compliance calendar and standard document repository.
SOC

Social & labour risk

Turnover, exclusion, grievances and workforce instability.

Watch forAbsenteeism, wage disputes and parallel informal practices.Respond withRole clarity, grievance channels, welfare and worker inclusion.
